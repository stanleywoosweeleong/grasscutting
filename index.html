For durian growers

Build soil by cutting, not spraying

Published research backs each point below. Tap one for the evidence and what to do with it.

    The science

    How grass and soil shape each other

    1

    Monocot grasses vs dicot ground covers

    ›

    Monocots carry one cotyledon (seed leaf) in the seed and dicots carry two (单子叶 / 双子叶). Every true grass (family Poaceae) is a monocot. The dicots used as ground covers are broadleaf plants such as white clover 白车轴草, dichondra 马蹄金 and perennial peanut 蔓花生.

    Roots
    MonocotFibrous mat from the crown
    DicotTaproot, plus roots at creeping nodes

    A fibrous mat copes with thin or compact soil. A taproot needs loose, deep soil.

    Leaves
    MonocotNarrow, parallel veins
    DicotBroad, net veins

    Broad leaves lose more water, so they need steadier moisture.

    Growing point
    MonocotAt the crown, near the soil
    DicotAt shoot tips, above ground

    Cutting removes dicot tips but spares grass crowns.

    Photosynthesis
    MonocotMostly C4 (tropical turf)
    DicotC3

    C4 makes more growth per unit of water and nitrogen.

    Legumes such as 蔓花生 add one twist: they make their own nitrogen, so what they need from fertile soil is phosphorus, potassium and moisture (section 3).

    Source: Sage & Pearcy 1987

    2

    What grows is the soil's report card

    ›

    What grows naturally reflects the soil's water, nutrients, structure and disturbance history. Ecologists call these indicator plants. Fields dominated by 茅草 in West Africa, for example, had degraded, low-fertility soils.

    The report card also feeds back. Plants change their soil through dead roots, root exudates, the microbes they host and the nutrients they pull up. The changed soil then favours different plants. This plant–soil feedback drives succession.

    You can't pick a grass to fix soil overnight. Managing the vegetation that grows there, mainly by how you cut it, is how the soil improves over the years.

    Sources: Chikoye et al. 2000 · van der Putten et al. 2013

    3

    Why 蔓花生 wins on poor soil and loses on rich soil

    ›

    Perennial peanut (Arachis pintoi) has an edge only where soil nitrogen is short. On fertile soil that edge switches off, and faster grasses shade it out.

    1. Fixing nitrogen costs energy. Root-nodule bacteria turn air nitrogen into ammonia, paid for with the plant's sugars. That costs more than taking nitrate from the soil.
    2. On low-nitrogen soil the cost pays off. Grasses are held back by the shortage; the legume is not.
    3. On rich soil, fixation switches down. Soil nitrate suppresses nodules, so the legume loses its edge.
    4. Then it competes for light and loses. C4 grasses grow taller and faster in heat. In mixed pastures, nitrogen fertilizer reliably lowers the legume share.

    On a fertile, fertilized orchard floor, the natural vegetation is grass, and 蔓花生 is working against it.

    Sources: Gutschick 1981 · Vance & Heichel 1991 · Streeter & Wong 1988 · Ledgard & Steele 1992 · Lascano et al. 2005

    4

    蔓花生 vs 茅草 on sandy soil

    ›

    On dry sand, cogongrass (Imperata cylindrica, 茅草) almost always wins. Shade is what pushes it back.

    Below ground
    茅草Dense rhizomes; regrows after cutting and fire
    蔓花生Taproot and stolons; slow to establish
    Drought on sand
    茅草Tolerates it
    蔓花生Fixation and growth slow down
    Weakness
    茅草Shade
    蔓花生Being overtopped

    Shade from tree fallows or tall cover crops such as velvetbean (Mucuna) suppresses 茅草. 蔓花生 grows too low to do that. In an orchard, the closing tree canopy does the job over time.

    Sources: MacDonald 2004 · MacDicken et al. 1996 · Akobundu et al. 2000

    5

    Roots build more soil than leaves

    ›

    Carbon from roots stays in soil about 2.4 times longer than carbon from leaves and stems. Microbes also turn underground inputs into stable organic matter more efficiently than surface litter.

    Roots build soil in four ways:

    • Organic matter. Dead roots and root sugars feed microbes, whose remains bind to clay as stable carbon.
    • Pores. Growing roots push soil apart; rotted roots leave channels for air, water, earthworms and new roots.
    • Glue. Fine roots, fungal threads and the fungal protein glomalin bind clay into crumbs that drain instead of sealing.
    • Chemistry and soil life. Roots free locked phosphorus, lift nutrients from depth and feed the soil food web.

    Cut high to keep roots growing. Removing more than about half of a grass's leaf area stopped root growth in a classic USDA study. Occasional cuts feed the soil; scalping shrinks the roots that build it.

    Sources: Rasse et al. 2005 · Sokol & Bradford 2019 · Cresswell & Kirkegaard 1995 · Tisdall & Oades 1982 · Rillig 2004 · Crider 1955 (USDA Tech. Bull. 1102)

    6

    Cutting vs herbicide

    ›

    Cutting beats herbicide for soil building because it keeps roots alive. A systemic herbicide such as glyphosate kills the whole plant, roots included. Those dead roots add organic matter once, and then the soil gets nothing until something regrows.

    Roots
    CuttingAlive; shed and regrow each cut
    HerbicideDie once
    Soil cover
    CuttingAlways covered
    HerbicideBare; crusting and erosion
    Organic matter
    CuttingSmall pulses for years
    HerbicideOne pulse

    Pulling or digging is worse than either, because it also breaks up the soil crumbs.

    Source: Sokol & Bradford 2019

    7

    Why regular cutting ends in cow grass

    ›

    Frequent cutting acts as a filter. Only low, creeping grasses survive it, so diversity drops and cow grass (Axonopus compressus, 地毯草) takes over. The shift is gradual: a mixed sward can take several years of regular cutting to become mostly cow grass.

    • Who survives: cow grass spreads by stolons with its growing points below cutting height. It suits moist, slightly acidic soils and tolerates part shade.
    • The pattern: species richness peaks at moderate disturbance. Very frequent cutting leaves only tolerant species; rare cutting lets a few tall ones dominate.
    • Direct evidence: cutting urban lawns less often increased plant diversity.

    A uniform cow-grass mat is easy, covers soil well and grows slowly. A mixed sward has roots at more depths and more soil life.

    Sources: CABI · Grime 1973 · Connell 1978 · Chollet et al. 2018

    8

    Putting it into practice

    ›

    Cut, leave the cuttings, cut higher, and reduce fertilizer step by step. Each step lowers both fertilizer and cutting costs.

    • Leave the clippings. Returning clippings substantially reduced the nitrogen fertilizer turf needed. Rake surplus into tree rings as mulch.
    • Cut higher. About 10–15 cm is a reasonable working height for an orchard floor (a suggestion, not from a study).
    • Reduce fertilizer gradually and watch whether the cow grass stays green and dense.
    • Time cuts against seeding to shift the mix without herbicide.
    • Use zones for diversity: paths and tree rings short, the rest cut less often.
    • On 茅草 patches, use shade and tall cover crops, not more cutting alone.
    • Don't pull or dig.

    Durian induction season is the exception. See the Durian tab.

    Sources: Kopp & Guillard 2002 · Harivandi & Gibeault 1999

    Durian orchards

    Two seasons on the orchard floor

    Durian flowering needs a stress signal from the root zone. After the leaf flush matures, the root zone must dry out and the ground must reach a certain temperature. A thick grass mat and mulch keep it moist and cool.

    Most of the year: build soil

    Before and during induction: let the root zone dry and warm

    Published guidance emphasises a dry spell after the leaf flush (CABI: Durio zibethinus). This approach works with that natural signal, with no growth regulators. Ticks are saved on this phone only.
